## Naive Bayes Algorithm

We continue introducing algorithms for solving classification tasks. This chapter covers a probabilistic model called the Bayes classifier. Bayes classifiers are a general term for a family of classification algorithms, all based on Bayes' theorem, and are thus collectively referred to as Bayes classifiers. Before introducing Bayes' theorem, let us tell a story: from 2015 to 2020, a woman surnamed Li, relying on her analysis of whether flights would be delayed, purchased approximately 900 flight delay insurance policies and received delay compensation, accumulating over 3 million yuan in total claims -- truly turning "flight delays into a fortune." Of course, this clever operation itself is not the focus of our discussion. Our question is: how did Ms. Li decide whether to purchase delay insurance? The main causes of flight delays are weather (including weather at both departure and arrival locations), airports (departure and arrival airports), and airlines. Because Ms. Li had experience working in aviation services, she had access to airport and airline-related data (weather data is relatively easier to obtain). By gathering the relevant data and applying Bayes' theorem, she could calculate the probability of a flight being delayed and decide whether to purchase delay insurance. Then, Ms. Li purchased tickets under various fabricated identities and bought large amounts of insurance (30 to 40 delay insurance policies per identity), so that once a flight was delayed, she could file claims with insurance companies. So, what we want to explore is how Bayes' theorem uses existing data to calculate the probability of a flight delay.

### Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem is an important theorem in probability theory that describes how to update our belief about the probability of an event occurring, starting from subjective experience or known facts, through collected sample data (evidence). The mathematical expression of Bayes' theorem is:

$$
P(A \vert B) = \frac{P(B \vert A)}{P(B)} \cdot P(A)
$$

Here, $\small{P(A)}$ is the **prior probability** of event $\small{A}$, which can be understood as known facts or subjective experience (**subjective probability**); $\small{P(B \vert A)}$ is the conditional probability of event $\small{B}$ given that event $\small{A}$ has occurred, commonly referred to as the **likelihood**; $\small{P(B)}$ is the (marginal) probability of event $\small{B}$, and both of these probabilities can be obtained from our collected sample data (evidence); $\small{P(A \vert B)}$ is the conditional probability of event $\small{A}$ given that event $\small{B}$ has occurred, i.e., the updated belief about the probability of event $\small{A}$ after collecting sample data, called the **posterior probability**. Bayes' theorem tells us an important fact: starting from known facts or subjective experience, we can update our understanding of the probability of an event through collected evidence. In simple terms, **we can infer unknown truths from known facts and collected evidence**.

Returning to Ms. Li's flight delay insurance example, suppose the current flight is from Chengdu Shuangliu International Airport to Beijing Capital International Airport, the airline is Sichuan Airlines, the departure weather is rainy (light rain), temperature 8°C, northeast wind level 2, and the arrival weather is sunny, temperature 4°C, northwest wind level 2. To make Bayes' theorem easier to understand, we simplify the conditions here by keeping only the precipitation information from the weather and temporarily ignoring other factors like temperature and wind speed. Corresponding to Bayes' theorem above:

$$
P(Delay \vert Departure=Shuangliu,Arrival=Capital,DepartWeather=LightRain,ArrivalWeather=Sunny,Airline=SichuanAir) = \\\\
\frac{P(Departure=Shuangliu,Arrival=Capital,DepartWeather=LightRain,ArrivalWeather=Sunny,Airline=SichuanAir \vert Delay)}{P(Departure=Shuangliu,Arrival=Capital,DepartWeather=LightRain,ArrivalWeather=Sunny,Airline=SichuanAir)} \cdot P(Delay)
$$

The left side of the equation above is the probability of the current flight being delayed that Ms. Li wants to know. The right side is essentially historical data and current information. The key to calculating this probability is computing the likelihood, i.e., what exactly is $\small{P(Departure=Shuangliu,Arrival=Capital,DepartWeather=LightRain,ArrivalWeather=Sunny,Airline=SichuanAir \vert Delay)}$? How do we calculate this conditional probability?

### Naive Bayes

The Naive Bayes algorithm is a classification algorithm based on Bayes' theorem and the feature conditional independence assumption. It is widely used due to its simplicity and efficiency. The key to the Naive Bayes algorithm lies in the word "naive," which refers to the feature conditional independence assumption we just mentioned. The conditional independence assumption states that the features used for classification are all independent given the class, and this assumption makes learning with Naive Bayes possible.

Suppose we have a feature set $\small{X = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}}$ and a class $\small{C}$. The Naive Bayes algorithm assumes:

$$
P(X \vert C) = P(x_1 \vert C) \cdot P(x_2 \vert C) \cdot \ldots \cdot P(x_n \vert C)
$$

This assumption greatly simplifies computational complexity, allowing us to only compute the probability of each feature given the class, without considering interactions between features. Applying this to the flight delay insurance example above, we can compute the likelihood as follows:

$$
P(Departure=Shuangliu,Arrival=Capital,DepartWeather=LightRain,ArrivalWeather=Sunny,Airline=SichuanAir \vert Delay) = \\\\
P(Departure=Shuangliu \vert Delay) \times P(Arrival=Capital \vert Delay) \times P(DepartWeather=LightRain \vert Delay) \times P(ArrivalWeather=Sunny \vert Delay) \times P(Airline=SichuanAir \vert Delay)
$$

### Algorithm Principles

#### Training Phase

During the training phase, the Naive Bayes algorithm first needs to compute the prior probability of each class and the conditional probability of each feature under each class.

1. **Compute prior probabilities**:

$$
P(C_{i}) = \frac{n_{i}}{n}
$$

Here, $\small{C_{i}}$ represents the class, $\small{n_{i}}$ is the number of samples in class $\small{C_{i}}$, and $\small{n}$ is the total sample size.

2. **Compute conditional probabilities**:

$$
P(x_{j} \vert C_{i}) = \frac{n_{i,j}}{n_{i}}
$$

Here, $\small{n_{i,j}}$ is the number of times feature $\small{x_{j}}$ appears in class $\small{C_{i}}$.

#### Prediction Phase

During the prediction phase, given a sample $\small{X}$ to be classified, the Naive Bayes algorithm computes the posterior probability of it belonging to each class through the following steps:

$$
P(C_{i} \vert X) = \frac{P(X \vert C_{i})}{P(X)} \cdot P(C_{i})
$$

In the formula above, $\small{P(X)}$ is a constant for each class and can be ignored. Combined with the independence assumption:

$$
P(C_{i} \vert X) \propto P(C_{i}) \cdot P(x_1 \vert C_{i}) \cdot P(x_2 \vert C_{i}) \cdot \ldots \cdot P(x_n \vert C_{i})
$$

This way, we can select the class with the highest posterior probability as the prediction result.

#### Code Implementation

We still use the Iris dataset as an example. Following the principles explained above, we implement a Naive Bayes classifier with NumPy. We start by loading the data, as shown below.

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, train_size=0.8, random_state=3)
```

During the training phase, we need to obtain class labels and their corresponding prior probabilities, and also compute the likelihood. The likelihood calculation uses the "naive" assumption mentioned above. We perform simple discretization of the continuous feature values of the Iris dataset (don't worry about whether this approach is reasonable for now), as shown below.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def naive_bayes_fit(X, y):
    """
    :param X: Sample features
    :param Y: Sample labels
    :returns: Tuple - (prior probabilities, likelihoods)
    """
    # Compute prior probabilities
    clazz_labels, clazz_counts = np.unique(y, return_counts=True)
    prior_probs = pd.Series({k: v / y.size for k, v in zip(clazz_labels, clazz_counts)})
    # Copy the array to create a duplicate
    X = np.copy(X)
    # Dictionary to store likelihood computation results
    likelihoods = {}
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):  # Loop over features
        # Equal-width binning for features (discretization)
        X[:, j] = pd.cut(X[:, j], bins=5, labels=np.arange(1, 6))
        for i in prior_probs.index:
            # Split data by label class and count frequency of each feature value
            x_prime = X[y == i, j]
            x_values, x_counts = np.unique(x_prime, return_counts=True)
            for k, value in enumerate(x_values):  # Loop over different feature values
                # Compute likelihood and store in dictionary (key is a triple - (label, feature index, feature value))
                likelihoods[(i, j, value)] = x_counts[k] / x_prime.size
    return prior_probs, likelihoods
```

By calling the function above, we get a tuple. After unpacking, the first element is the prior probability corresponding to class label $\small{C_{i}}$, and the second is the likelihood that the $\small{j}$-th feature takes a certain `value` under class $\small{C_{i}}$ (in the code above, we use pandas' `cut` function to bin the feature values, where `value` ranges from `1` to `5`). The former is a `Series` object and the latter is a `dict` object, as shown below:

```python
p_ci, p_x_ci = naive_bayes_fit(X_train, y_train)

print('Likelihoods: ', p_x_ci, sep='\n')
```

During the prediction phase, we use the prior probabilities and likelihoods obtained from the function above to compute posterior probabilities, then assign predicted class labels to samples based on the maximum posterior probability.

```python
def naive_bayes_predict(X, p_ci, p_x_ci):
    """
    Naive Bayes classifier prediction
    :param X: Sample features
    :param p_ci: Prior probabilities
    :param p_x_ci: Likelihoods
    :return: Predicted labels
    """
    # Equal-width binning for features (discretization)
    X = np.copy(X)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        X[:, j] = pd.cut(X[:, j], bins=5, labels=np.arange(1, 6))
    # 2D array to store posterior probabilities for each sample and each class
    results = np.zeros((X.shape[0], p_ci.size))
    clazz_labels = p_ci.index.values
    for k in range(X.shape[0]):
        for i, label in enumerate(clazz_labels):
            # Get prior probability (result from training)
            prob = p_ci.loc[label]
            # Compute posterior probability after obtaining feature data
            for j in range(X.shape[1]):
                # If there is no corresponding likelihood, use 0
                prob *= p_x_ci.get((i, j, X[k, j]), 0)
            results[k, i] = prob
    # Select prediction label based on the class with the highest probability for each sample
    return clazz_labels[results.argmax(axis=1)]
```

The two functions above are intended to help you understand the working principles of Naive Bayes. In practice, we recommend using the classes provided in scikit-learn's `naive_bayes` module to create Naive Bayes models. This module contains five variants of the Naive Bayes algorithm, each targeting different types of data and feature distributions. The five corresponding Naive Bayes classifiers are: `BernoulliNB`, `CategoricalNB`, `ComplementNB`, `GaussianNB`, and `MultinomialNB`. Their differences are shown in the table below:

| Classifier      | Feature Type    | Main Assumption                                        |
| --------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| `BernoulliNB`   | Binary features | Features follow a Bernoulli distribution               |
| `CategoricalNB` | Categorical features | Features follow a multinomial distribution, commonly used for categorical data |
| `ComplementNB`  | Count features  | Uses complement probabilities, commonly used for imbalanced datasets |
| `GaussianNB`    | Continuous features | Features follow a Gaussian distribution              |
| `MultinomialNB` | Count features  | Features follow a multinomial distribution, commonly used for text classification |

For the Iris dataset, since its features are continuous values, we can use `GaussianNB` to create the model, as shown below.

```python
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB

model = GaussianNB()
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = model.predict(X_test)
```

### Advantages and Disadvantages

The advantages of the Naive Bayes algorithm include:

1. **Simple logic, easy to implement, and suitable for large-scale datasets**.
2. **Low computational overhead**. The probabilities needed for prediction are all prepared during the training phase. When new data arrives, we only need to retrieve the corresponding probability values and perform simple calculations to get the prediction result.
3. **Less affected by noise and irrelevant attributes**.

Of course, due to the "feature independence" assumption, the disadvantages of the Naive Bayes algorithm are also quite obvious. In practical applications, features are rarely completely independent, especially in high-dimensional data. If the correlation between features is large, the classification performance will become poor. To address this issue, several new methods have been derived from the Naive Bayes algorithm, including: Semi-Naive Bayes (One Dependent Estimator), AODE (Averaged One Dependent Estimator), K-Dependent Naive Bayes, Naive Bayes Networks, Gaussian Mixture Naive Bayes, etc. Interested readers can explore these on their own.

The Naive Bayes algorithm has wide applications in multiple fields. Here are some common application scenarios:
