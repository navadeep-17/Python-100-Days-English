## Ensemble Learning Algorithms

In previous chapters, we mainly introduced single models in machine learning. In fact, combining multiple single models into a comprehensive model has long become the mainstream approach adopted by modern machine learning, and this method is called **ensemble learning**. The goal of ensemble learning is to build strong learners by combining multiple weak learners (models that perform slightly better than random guessing; if too strong, they are prone to overfitting), thereby overcoming the limitations of individual models and achieving **better generalization ability** than single models. It is commonly used in **scenarios requiring high-precision predictions**.

### Algorithm Classification

Ensemble learning algorithms are mainly divided into the following categories:

1. **Bagging**: Generates multiple data subsets by random sampling from the training data, trains multiple models on these subsets, and combines their results. The principle of this ensemble learning approach is shown in the figure below. The most typical example is the random forest we discussed earlier.

    <img src="res/07_ensemble_bagging.png" style="zoom:50%;">

2. **Boosting**: Iteratively trains multiple models, with each training round focusing on the samples that were incorrectly predicted in the previous round. Each new model's training objective is to compensate for the shortcomings of the previous round's model. The principle of this ensemble learning approach is shown in the figure below. Classic algorithms include AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost.

    <img src="res/07_ensemble_boosting.png" style="zoom:50%;">

    The basic principle of Boosting is: initially, all samples are assigned equal weights; when training the first model, the weights of misclassified samples are increased; when training the next model, more attention is paid to samples misclassified by previous models; finally, the results of all models are weighted and combined (better-performing models receive higher weights) to produce the final output. In simple terms, Boosting is the process of serially training a series of weak classifiers, giving more attention to samples misclassified by earlier weak classifiers in subsequent ones, and finally combining these classifiers into an optimal strong classifier.

3. **Stacking**: Trains multiple models and uses their prediction results as new features fed into another model (usually called the "second-level model"), which makes the final prediction. The principle is shown in the figure below.

    <img src="res/07_ensemble_stacking.png" style="zoom:36%;">

### AdaBoost

AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) was proposed by Yoav Freund and Robert Schapire in 1996 and is a classic ensemble learning algorithm, commonly translated as the adaptive boosting algorithm. AdaBoost's approach is quite straightforward: first, **increase the weights of samples misclassified by the previous round's weak classifier**, and second, perform a linear combination of multiple weak classifiers, **giving higher weights to better-performing weak classifiers**. Its adaptiveness is reflected in adjusting sample weights based on the errors of the previous round's model.

The training process of the AdaBoost algorithm is step-by-step iterative. The key steps are as follows:

1. **Initialize sample weights**: Assign an equal initial weight to each training sample. For $\small{N}$ samples, the initial weight is:

$$
w_{i}^{(1)} = \frac{1}{N}, \ i = 1, 2, \cdots, N
$$

Here, $\small{w_{i}^{(1)}}$ represents the weight of the $\small{i}$-th sample, which is initially equal.

2. **Train weak learners**: In each iteration round, AdaBoost trains a weak classifier (e.g., a decision stump, i.e., a decision tree with depth 1) based on the current sample weights. The weak classifier aims to minimize the weighted error. For the classifier model (weak learner) $\small{h_t}$ obtained in round $\small{t}$, compute its weighted error:

$$
\varepsilon_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{i}^{(t)} \cdot I(y_{i} \neq h_{t}(x_{i}))
$$

Here, $\small{y_{i}}$ is the true label of the $\small{i}$-th sample, $\small{h_{t}(x_{i})}$ is the prediction result of the round $\small{t}$ model $\small{h_{t}}$ for the $\small{i}$-th sample $\small{x_{i}}$ (taking values of 1 or -1), and $\small{I(y_{i} \neq h_{t}(x_{i}))}$ is the indicator function, which equals 1 when sample $\small{x_{i}}$ is misclassified, and 0 otherwise.

3. **Update classifier weights**: Compute the weight $\small{\alpha_{t}}$ of the round $\small{t}$ classifier model and use it to update each sample's weight. The formula for the classifier weight $\small{\alpha_{t}}$ is:

$$
\alpha_{t} = \frac{1}{2} ln \left( \frac{1 - \varepsilon_{t}}{\varepsilon_{t}} \right)
$$

When the classifier's error is low, $\small{\alpha_{t}}$ has a larger value, meaning the classifier receives a higher weight.

4. **Update sample weights**: Update sample weights based on the current classifier's performance. Misclassified samples receive increased weights, while correctly classified samples receive decreased weights. The sample weight update formula is:

$$
w_{i}^{(t + 1)} = w_{i}^{(t)} \cdot e^{-\alpha_{t} y_{i} h_{t}(x_{i})}
$$

5. **Normalize weights**: Normalize all sample weights so that the total weight sums to 1.

6. **Final classifier**: AdaBoost's final classifier is a weighted combination of all weak learners. During prediction, the final class is determined by weighted voting:

$$
H(x) = \text{sign} \left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_{t} h_{t}(x) \right)
$$

Here, $\small{\text{sign}}$ is the sign function, defined as follows:

$$
\text{sign}(z) = \begin{cases} +1 \ (z \ge 0) \\\\ -1 \ (z \lt 0) \end{cases}
$$

For example, if there are 3 weak learners $\small{h_{1}(x)}$, $\small{h_{2}(x)}$, $\small{h_{3}(x)}$, with outputs `+1`, `-1`, and `+1` respectively, and corresponding weights $\small{\alpha_{1} = 0.5}$, $\small{\alpha_{2} = 0.3}$, $\small{\alpha_{3} = 0.2}$, then the weighted sum is:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{3} \alpha_{t} h_{t}(x) = 0.5 \times 1 + 0.3 \times -1 + 0.2 \times 1 = 0.4
$$

Since the weighted sum `0.4` is positive, the sign function outputs `+1`, meaning the final predicted class is positive.

We still use the Iris dataset as an example to apply the AdaBoost ensemble learning algorithm to build a classification model. The complete code is shown below.

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

# Dataset loading and splitting
iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, train_size=0.8, random_state=3)

# Initialize weak classifier (decision stump)
base_estimator = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1)
# Initialize AdaBoost classifier
model = AdaBoostClassifier(base_estimator, n_estimators=50)
# Train the model
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
# Predict results
y_pred = model.predict(X_test)

# Output evaluation report
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred))
```

weighted avg       0.93      0.93      0.93        30

Here are some important hyperparameter settings to note:

1. `n_estimators`: Specifies the number of base learners (weak classifiers) to train, default value is 50.
2. `learning_rate`: Controls the contribution of each base learner to the final model, i.e., the learning rate, default value is 1.0.
3. `algorithm`: Determines the AdaBoost training algorithm. AdaBoost has two main training modes:
    - `'SAMME'`: Algorithm for multi-class classification, using an additive model.
    - `'SAMME.R'`: Algorithm based on Real AdaBoost, for weighted binary and multi-class classification, using a weighted resampling strategy.
4. `base_estimator`: The base learner, default value is `None`, which means using a `DecisionTreeClassifier` with `max_depth=1` as the base learner. Therefore, both parameters in the code above for creating the `AdaBoostClassifier` object can be omitted, since they are exactly the default values.

Increasing `n_estimators` can improve model performance but may lead to overfitting; increasing `learning_rate` can speed up training but may make the model unstable. Cross-validation is usually needed to find the optimal combination of `n_estimators` and `learning_rate`. For most problems, `DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1)` is a common choice, but for more complex data, other base learners such as `SVC` or `LogisticRegression` can be considered.

### GBDT

GBDT (Gradient Boosting Decision Trees) is also a powerful ensemble learning algorithm. Compared to AdaBoost, GBDT-based models are more widely used. GBDT is based on the **gradient boosting** idea, combining the advantages of decision trees, progressively improving the model through a series of weak classifiers (decision trees). Each training step reduces the error of the previous model (fitting residuals) to improve prediction performance.

GBDT uses gradient descent to minimize the loss function. For regression tasks, the loss function is typically mean squared error (MSE); for classification tasks, the commonly used loss function is log loss. Below, we use a binary classification task as an example to explain the algorithm principles.

1. Loss function. In classification tasks, the log-likelihood loss function is typically used. For binary classification, the loss function is as follows:

$$
L(y, F(x)) = -y\log(p(x)) - (1 - y)\log(1 - p(x))
$$

Here, $\small{y}$ is the actual label ($\small{y \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace}$, representing class 0 or 1), and $\small{p(x)}$ is the model's predicted probability that sample $\small{x}$ belongs to class 1. Since GBDT is based on the gradient boosting algorithm, in each round of updates, we optimize this loss function through gradient descent.

2. Gradient computation. To use gradient boosting, we need to compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the current model's prediction. Let the current model's output be $\small{F(x)}$, i.e., the prediction function. According to the log loss function, $\small{p(x)}$ is the probability obtained by transforming the model output $\small{F(x)}$, typically using the Sigmoid function:

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-F(x)}}
$$

When computing the gradient of the loss function with respect to $\small{F(x)}$, we get:

$$
\frac{\partial{L(y, F(x))}}{\partial{F(x)}} = p(x) - y
$$

The gradient is $\small{p(x) - y}$, which tells us the gap between the current model's prediction $\small{F(x)}$ and the true label $\small{y}$.

3. Model update. Each round of update includes two steps:

    - Compute residuals: In each iteration, we compute the residual of the current model, i.e., $\small{\delta_i = p(x_i) - y_i}$, representing the error for each sample.
    - Fit residuals: Use a new base learner (typically a decision tree) to fit these residuals. In classification tasks, the decision tree we train does not fit the true labels but fits the residuals, i.e., the prediction error of the current model.

The update rule is:

$$
F_{m + 1}(x) = F_{m}(x) + \eta h_{m}(x)
$$

Here, $\small{F_{m}(x)}$ is the output of the round $\small{m}$ model; $\small{h_{m}(x)}$ is the weak learner (typically a decision tree) trained in round $\small{m}$, which predicts the current model's residuals; $\small{\eta}$ is the learning rate, controlling the contribution of each tree. Through this gradual residual fitting, the final model $\small{F(x)}$ is a strong learner composed of multiple decision trees.

For multi-class tasks, we need to extend the loss function and gradient computation accordingly. The commonly used multi-class loss function is the multinomial log loss. For each class $\small{k}$, the loss function can be expressed as:

$$
L(y, F(x)) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_{k} \log(p_{k}(x))
$$

Here, $\small{K}$ is the total number of classes, $\small{y_{k}}$ is the indicator function for target class $\small{k}$, and $\small{p_k(x)}$ is the predicted probability that sample $\small{x}$ belongs to class $\small{k}$.

We still use the Iris dataset as an example to apply the GBDT ensemble learning algorithm to build a classification model. The complete code is shown below.

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

# Dataset loading and splitting
iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, train_size=0.8, random_state=3)

# Initialize GBDT classifier
model = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=32)
# Train the model
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
# Predict results
y_pred = model.predict(X_test)

# Output evaluation report
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred))
```

Let us discuss several important hyperparameters of `GradientBoostingClassifier`:

1. `loss`: Specifies the loss function used for the classification task, default value is `deviance`, which uses the log loss function. You can also choose `exponential`, which uses the exponential loss function (AdaBoost's loss function).
2. `learning_rate`: Controls the influence of each tree on the final prediction, default value is 0.1. A smaller learning rate usually provides better generalization but requires more weak classifiers (i.e., more trees) to fit the training data; if the learning rate is set too large, it may cause underfitting.
3. `n_estimators`: Specifies the number of base learners (usually decision trees) to train, default value is 100.
4. `subsample`: Controls the proportion of data used for training each tree, default value is 1.0 (using all data). By setting a value less than 1, a random subset of samples can be selected for each tree, increasing model randomness and reducing overfitting. Typically, 0.8 or 0.9 are good choices.
5. `criterion`: Controls the split criterion, default value is `'friedman_mse'`. This parameter determines how to select the best split at each node. Options include:
    - `'friedman_mse'`: An improved version of MSE that reduces sensitivity to imbalanced data and can produce better results in some practical tasks.
    - `'mse'`: Uses the traditional mean squared error to evaluate the quality of each split point. Lower MSE means better splitting at the current node. For classification tasks, `'friedman_mse'` often achieves better results.
    - `'mae'`: Uses mean absolute error to evaluate split quality. MAE is less sensitive to outliers but is typically used less frequently in practice.
6. `validation_fraction`: Specifies the proportion of validation set during the training process, used for early stopping to avoid overfitting. This parameter works with `n_iter_no_change` to evaluate performance on the validation set; training stops early when there is no improvement for several consecutive rounds.

In addition to the hyperparameters mentioned above, there are some similar to those of decision trees, which we will not elaborate on here. Note that when increasing `n_estimators` (number of trees), you typically need to decrease `learning_rate` to prevent overfitting; adjusting the decision tree's `max_depth` and `min_samples_split` can also reduce tree complexity to prevent overfitting; using `subsample` and `early_stopping` parameters can also achieve similar effects. We recommend finding the best parameter combination through grid search cross-validation, combined with training and validation errors.

### XGBoost

Looking at GBDT from performance metrics such as algorithm accuracy, speed, and generalization ability, there is still significant room for optimization. XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) is a top-tier gradient boosting model based on GBDT, proposed by Tianqi Chen in his paper [*XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.02754). Compared to GBDT, in terms of algorithm accuracy, XGBoost expands the loss function to second-order derivatives, enabling the gradient boosting tree model to better approximate the true loss; in terms of algorithm speed, XGBoost uses weighted quantile sketch and sparsity-aware algorithms, along with cache optimization and model parallelism to improve speed; in terms of generalization ability, it prevents overfitting through adding regularization terms to the loss function, setting shrinkage rates in the additive model, and column subsampling. Due to these reasons, XGBoost is very popular and widely used in academia, industry, and competitions alike. For details about XGBoost, interested readers can read Tianqi Chen's paper; we will not elaborate further here.

We can use XGBoost as follows. First, install the dependency.

```bash
pip install xgboost
```

The following code demonstrates how to use XGBoost.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import xgboost as xgb
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

# Dataset loading and splitting
iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, train_size=0.8, random_state=3)

# Convert data to DMatrix format
dm_train = xgb.DMatrix(X_train, y_train)
dm_test = xgb.DMatrix(X_test)

# Set model parameters
params = {
    'booster': 'gbtree',           # Type of base learner used for training
    'objective': 'multi:softmax',  # Model loss function
    'num_class': 3,                # Number of classes
    'gamma': 0.1,                  # Minimum loss reduction required for each split
    'max_depth': 6,                # Maximum depth of decision tree
    'lambda': 2,                   # L2 regularization weight
    'subsample': 0.8,              # Proportion of samples randomly selected for each tree
    'colsample_bytree': 0.8,      # Feature selection proportion for each tree or node
    'eta': 0.001,                  # Learning rate
    'seed': 10,                    # Random seed
    'nthread': 16,                 # Number of parallel threads for training
}

# Train the model
model = xgb.train(params, dm_train, num_boost_round=200)
# Predict results
y_pred = model.predict(dm_test)

# Output model evaluation report
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred))

# Plot feature importance scores
xgb.plot_importance(model)
plt.grid(False)
plt.show()
```

Let us discuss the XGBoost model parameters. First, let's look at the `param` dictionary in the code above. Several parameters need detailed explanation.

1. `booster`: Specifies the type of base learner for training, default value is `'gbtree'`, indicating the use of traditional decision trees as base learners. Other options include `'gblinear'` and `'dart'`, where the former uses linear regression or logistic regression, and the latter is also based on decision trees but has a tree-dropping mechanism to reduce overfitting risk. Typically, `'gbtree'` is suitable for most problems, especially those involving nonlinear relationships; `'dart'` is suitable for complex datasets, especially when overfitting occurs. For smaller datasets or stronger linear relationships, try `'gblinear'`.
2. `objective`: Specifies the model's loss function (optimization objective). Common options include:
    - `'reg:squarederror'`: Mean squared error (MSE) for regression tasks.
    - `'reg:logistic'`: Logistic regression for regression tasks, typically used for binary classification.
    - `'binary:logistic'`: Logistic regression for binary classification, outputs probability values.
    - `'binary:logitraw'`: Logistic regression for binary classification, outputs raw values before Sigmoid.
    - `'multi:softmax'`: Multi-class classification, outputs the class with maximum probability.
    - `'multi:softprob'`: Multi-class classification, outputs probability distribution for each class.
3. `eta`/`learning_rate`: Learning rate in XGBoost, default value is 0.3. It is recommended to set the initial value between 0.01 and 0.1.
4. `alpha` and `lambda`: The former controls the strength of L1 regularization (Lasso), default is `0`; the latter controls the strength of L2 regularization (Ridge), default is `1`.
5. `scale_pos_weight`: Used to handle class imbalance, especially in binary classification. In cases of severe class imbalance, adjusting this parameter increases the weight of the minority class, making the model pay more attention to minority class samples, default value is 1.
6. `gamma`: Controls the minimum loss function reduction required for each split. This parameter controls tree growth; larger `gamma` values make the tree smaller, reducing overfitting risk. Default value is 0, meaning the model is not restricted by splits, and trees will grow as deep as possible until nodes have insufficient samples.
7. `num_class`: Parameter for multi-class tasks, indicating the number of classes. Not needed for binary classification.
8. `colsample_bytree` / `colsample_bylevel` / `colsample_bynode`: Control the proportion of features sampled at each tree, each level, and each node. These parameters control model complexity. Smaller values increase model randomness, preventing overfitting; larger values mean each tree uses more features, potentially leading to overfitting.
    - `colsample_bytree`: Feature proportion per tree (default 1.0).
    - `colsample_bylevel`: Feature proportion per level (default 1.0).
    - `colsample_bynode`: Feature proportion per node (default 1.0).

In addition to the model parameters provided in the `param` dictionary, several parameters of the `train` function are also noteworthy as model hyperparameters.

1. `num_boost_round`: Number of tree training rounds. Setting a smaller `learning_rate` and increasing training rounds can improve model stability.
2. `early_stopping_rounds`: Implements early stopping. When the loss function on the validation set stops decreasing for the specified number of rounds, training automatically stops to avoid overfitting.
3. `feval`: Sets a user-defined evaluation function. This allows users to flexibly use any evaluation metric suitable for a specific task. Note that the evaluation function takes two parameters: one for model predictions (NumPy `ndarray` object) and one for training data (XGBoost `DMatrix` object); it returns a tuple `(name, value)`, where `name` is the evaluation metric name and `value` is the metric value.
4. `obj`: Sets a user-defined objective function. The objective function computes the gradient and second-order derivative at each step to guide the model optimization process. Interested readers can explore this on their own.
5. `evals`: Specifies one or more validation sets, as a list of `(data, label)` tuples, where each tuple represents an evaluation dataset and must be a `DMatrix` object. During training, XGBoost evaluates performance on the validation sets after each iteration, typically used to monitor overfitting or tune hyperparameters.
6. `eval_results`: Stores all evaluation results computed during training, typically passed as a dictionary.
7. `verbose_eval`: Controls the output frequency of evaluation results during training. Can be set to an integer indicating how many iterations between outputs, or `True` or `False` for output every round or no output.
8. `xgb_model`: Used to load a previously trained model to continue training from where it left off. You can specify an `xgb_model` file or pass a `Booster` object.
9. `callbacks`: Adds custom callback functions during training. Callbacks can provide additional control at each iteration, such as automatic training termination or learning rate adjustment.

### LightGBM

LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a top-tier Boosting algorithm framework open-sourced by Microsoft in 2017. Although it is essentially still a GBDT algorithm, it is designed for processing large-scale datasets, particularly in scenarios requiring high efficiency and low memory consumption. In terms of GBDT algorithm performance, XGBoost is already very efficient but not without flaws. LightGBM is an improved version addressing XGBoost's shortcomings, making the GBDT algorithm lighter, more efficient, and both faster and more accurate through four methods: histogram algorithm (binning continuous features), Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS, retaining samples with larger gradients during training to reduce computation), Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB, combining mutually exclusive features to reduce feature space dimensionality), and leaf-wise growth strategy (prioritizing splitting the current leaf node to expand tree depth). Of course, on smaller datasets, LightGBM's advantages are not obvious, and regardless of whether it is XGBoost or LightGBM, model interpretability remains an issue, and hyperparameter tuning difficulty is also considerable.

Here, we will not use more space to explain LightGBM's details. Interested readers can directly visit LightGBM's [official documentation](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/stable/index.html). We will directly demonstrate how to use LightGBM through code. First, install it using the following command.

```bash
pip install lightgbm
```

We still use the Iris dataset to train the model, as shown below.

```python
import lightgbm as lgb
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

# Load and split the dataset
iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, train_size=0.8, random_state=3)

# Convert data to LightGBM data format
train_data = lgb.Dataset(X_train, label=y_train)
test_data = lgb.Dataset(X_test, label=y_test, reference=train_data)

# Set model parameters
params = {
    'objective': 'multiclass',   # Multi-class classification problem
    'num_class': 3,              # Number of classes
    'metric': 'multi_logloss',   # Multi-class log loss function
    'boosting_type': 'gbdt',     # Use gradient boosting tree algorithm
    'num_leaves': 31,            # Number of leaf nodes
    'learning_rate': 0.05,       # Learning rate
    'feature_fraction': 0.75,    # Proportion of features randomly selected for each training
    'early_stopping_rounds': 10  # Number of consecutive rounds without performance improvement before stopping
}
# Model training
model = lgb.train(params=params, train_set=train_data, num_boost_round=200, valid_sets=[test_data])
# Model prediction
y_pred = model.predict(X_test, num_iteration=model.best_iteration)
# Convert prediction results to labels
y_pred_max = np.argmax(y_pred, axis=1)

# View model evaluation report
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred_max))
```

Here we briefly explain the model's hyperparameters. For more details, please refer to the official documentation.

1. `objective`: Sets the optimization objective function (loss function). Options include:
    - `'regression'`: Regression tasks.
    - `'binary'`: Binary classification tasks.
    - `'multiclass'`: Multi-class classification tasks.
    - `'multiclassova'`: Multi-class classification using one-vs-all strategy.
    - `'rank_xendcg'`, `'lambdarank'`: Ranking tasks.
2. `metric`: Evaluation metric for model performance. Options include:
    - `'l2'`, `'mean_squared_error'`: MSE for regression tasks.
    - `'binary_error'`: Binary classification error rate.
    - `'multi_logloss'`: Multi-class log loss.
    - `'auc'`: AUC for binary classification.
    - `'precision'`, `'recall'`, `'f1'`: Precision, recall, and F1 score.
3. `boosting_type`: Sets the boosting type. Options include:
    - `'gbdt'`: Traditional gradient boosting tree.
    - `'dart'`: Decision tree with random tree-dropping mechanism to prevent overfitting.
    - `'goss'`: Accelerates training through gradient-based one-side sampling.
    - `'rf'`: Random forest.
4. `num_leaves`/`max_depth`: Number of leaf nodes / maximum depth of the decision tree, controlling tree complexity.
5. `lambda_l1`/`lambda_l2`: L1 and L2 regularization parameters, controlling model complexity and preventing overfitting.
6. `max_bin`: Maximum number of bins for splitting continuous features (data binning).
7. `feature_fraction`: Proportion of features randomly selected for each training.
8. `early_stopping_rounds`: Sets the number of consecutive rounds without improvement in evaluation metrics before training stops early.

### Summary

Ensemble learning reduces model bias and variance by combining multiple models, typically achieving better prediction results than single models. Since ensemble learning usually combines multiple base models, it can effectively reduce the overfitting problem that may exist in single models, handle anomalous and noisy data, and be more stable than single models. Of course, ensemble learning also has issues such as high computational cost, poor model interpretability, and complex hyperparameter tuning. In addition to XGBoost and LightGBM, there is another Boosting algorithm called CatBoost, famous for handling categorical features. All three are outstanding representatives of the GBDT algorithm family. Although deep learning, which relies on "brute force," is currently mainstream, Boosting algorithms represented by XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost still have broad application scenarios, even in modeling unstructured data (text, speech, images, video).
